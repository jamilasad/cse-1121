a[1] == a[4], then a[2] == a[3], then stop. The left index goes up by 1, the right one goes down by 1. We stop when they meet.

Step 1

    
The problem

    
Read a (n numbers) and b (m numbers). Copy a backwards, then b backwards, into c. Then delete the consecutive values: when a value comes again right after itself, keep only one. The result is d.

    

  

  
2

    
Step 2

    
Copy a into c — by hand, then a loop

    
Start simple: copy a into the first part of c, with no reverse. One line per box, then see what changes.

    

  

  
3

    
Step 3

    
Copy b into the second part of c

        
Your turn

      
c[0] to c[n-1] are full. Where does b[0] go? Can we write c[i] = b[i]?

      
      
b[0] goes to c[n]. c[i] = b[i] would write over the copy of a. Two numbers change now, so we use two variables: i for b, j for c.

    

    

  

  
4

    
Step 4

    
Now copy in reverse

        
Your turn

      
Copy a backwards: c[0] = a[3];, c[1] = a[2]; … The left number goes up. What does the right number do?

      
      
It goes down, like i and j in the palindrome.

    

    

  

  
5

    
Step 5

    
Delete the consecutive values

    
Keep the first value of each run, then jump over the rest of the run. i reads c, j writes d, k looks ahead.

    
Your turn

      
k stops on the first different value. Why do we write i = k - 1 and not i = k?

        
What does the for loop do after the body?

      
      
The for loop does i++ next. With i = k we would jump over one box.

    

    

  

  
6

    
Step 6

    
Another way

    
A shorter way looks back instead of ahead: keep c[i] when it is different from c[i - 1]. c[0] has no box before it, so it is always kept.

    

  

  
What we learned

    
    
      
  1. Write the first lines by hand, see what changes, then make the loop.
    2. 
      
  2. One variable, one job: i reads, j writes, k looks ahead.
    3. 
      
  3. If you change i inside a for loop, i++ still runs after the body.
    4.
